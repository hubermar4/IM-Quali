How the ranking is calculated

Formulas and assumptions
  1. Your ability per split on an average course. as = geometric mean over your results of ts × A(age group then) ÷ A(age group next race) ÷ fs(race-year) for s = swim, bike, run. fs are the split factors from the Experimental tab; A is the age-grading standard, so results from a younger age group are slowed down to your next age group. Transitions: your T1 + T2 relative to that race-year's median, averaged over your results.
  2. Expected time at race-year r. Tr = aswim·fswim(r) + abike·fbike(r) + arun·frun(r) + transition ratio × median T1+T2(r). A strong cyclist loses less time than the field on a hard bike course, so hilly courses rank higher for them. Without results: Tr = your average-course time × foverall(r).
  3. Time needed Nr in your age group: the slower of the age-group winner and the performance-pool equivalent, or the allocated age-group rank under historical legacy rules. The rank-factor scenario adds floor(slots × factor + 0.5) positions to the initial slots. Density and 15-absence scans require an original P1 race and compatible same-cycle matches. Missing P1 evidence can use a labelled strict fallback only when explicitly enabled. Reported cutoffs are separate historical evidence.
  4. Chance in race-year r. pr = Φ( ln(Nr ÷ Tr) ÷ σ ). Φ is the standard normal distribution; σ is how much one athlete's result varies from race to race after course and conditions are removed (the residual SD of the course model), widened by √(1 + 1/n) when your ability comes from n results.
  5. Chance at a race = average of pr over eligible past race-years. This what-if estimate is not a guaranteed likelihood; races with few eligible race-years are less certain.
  6. Course fit = your expected time ÷ the expected time of same-level athletes (median splits of your age group at your average-course level) on this course, relative to an average course. Negative means the course suits your profile.

Pool estimates are unverified Kona-starter matching heuristics, not observed acceptance or predicted future cutoffs. They apply only to covered full-distance editions, not all historical years or 70.3 races. Not modelled: ceremony attendance, prior qualification, future changes in field size or allocation, and whether your results were your first IRONMAN (about 1% slower).

Pool cutoffs and conditional skipped places

For original P1 races, the density estimate detects a drop in compatible championship matches; its minute-rounded range is not a confidence interval. The scan reports the last matched athlete before 15 consecutive unmatched gender-pool ranks after the fixed allocation. Legacy age-group races have no density or scan estimate. Missing matches, other qualifying races and special-entry routes can bias both methods. Neither proves acceptance or counts declined slots. Frankfurt 2026 uses a modified course, not a comparable full-distance finish time.

Reported last-accepted pool times are source-labeled references, separate from athlete matches. A time reported to the minute does not establish an exact pool rank. A match-based cutoff is unknown until a slot source is attributed. A single local finish does not establish that source: athletes may qualify elsewhere or enter through Legacy or another special route. Reviewed attributions are assumptions, not verified acceptances. Unverified modes show candidate maxima, not qualifying cutoffs. Skipped places = max(0, deepest attributed pool rank minus initial pool slots); prior qualification, automatic-slot roll-downs, reallocations and Kona DNS mean this is not a count of people who declined.

How the factors are calculated

Method, formulas, and fit statistics for the selected distance
  1. Match athletes across race-years. Identity = name tokens (accents removed, lower case, sorted, so "Anna Berg" = "BERG, Anna") + gender + country. An identity is dropped if it appears twice in one race-year (two different people), if its age groups imply inconsistent birth years, or if it has fewer than two race-years. Pros, results without an age group, and shortened or modified race-years are left out.
  2. Age-grade. t′ = t × A(gender, age group) with A from the —, so ageing from M40-44 to M45-49 between two races is not mistaken for a harder course.
  3. Fit, for each split separately:
    ln t′i,r = ai + cr + b · Ri,r + εi,r
    ai = ability of athlete i; cr = effect of race-year r; Ri,r = 1 if this is not the athlete's first race in the data, else 0; b = returning-athlete effect; ε = everything else. Least squares, solved by alternating means until nothing changes: ai = mean of (ln t′ − cr − b·R) over athlete i's races; cr = mean of (ln t′ − ai − b·R) over race-year r's athletes; b = mean of (ln t′ − ai − cr) over returning results. Because every athlete is compared only with themselves, ability cancels out.
  4. Remove outliers and refit. Results with |ε| above 4 × 1.4826 × median |ε| (a robust 4-σ limit: mechanicals, walked marathons, wrong matches) are dropped.
  5. Normalise. cr is shifted to average 0 over qualifying race-years. Race-year factor fr = ecr; f = 1.05 is shown as +5%.
  6. Split into course, season, and conditions. ln frace,year = Crace + Syear + conditions, fitted the same way over all race-years; seasons average 0 over qualifying race-years.
  7. Use. Course-adjusted time = t ÷ fr. A time T on an average course is expected to be T × fr at race-year r.

Bike course check: GPX model vs. athlete data

Bike difficulty relative to the mean of the compared courses. The GPX model uses one track per race and a 200 W reference rider; the athlete data is the season-adjusted bike course effect over all race-years. The model gets the order of courses mostly right but compresses the differences: it rides faster than a typical age-grouper, and it ignores road surface, wind, corners, and drafting. Noisy elevation also inflates flat coastal courses. The Lanzarote track ends about 175 m above its start (altimeter drift on the final descent), which changes its factor by about 0.4%.
